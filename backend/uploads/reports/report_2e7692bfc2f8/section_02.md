## Feature Viability Index: Core Value Anchors versus Discarded Utilities

The simulated trial of MagicBoard across more than 1,000 Brazilian educators demonstrates a sharp polarization in how teachers evaluate the platform's nine core features. Rather than a uniform reception, educators established a clear operational hierarchy: tools that automate laborious, off-hours bureaucratic obligations were hailed as essential lifelines, whereas features presuming frictionless classroom connectivity or duplicating entrenched free tools were either heavily modified or outright discarded. 

The resulting Feature Viability Index maps each capability along three primary evaluative axes: perceived usefulness, ease of execution in realistic classroom environments, and pedagogical trust.

**The Feature Viability Matrix**

Based on aggregate agent task simulations, the nine product modules sorted into three distinct viability tiers: Core Value Anchors, Context-Dependent Utilities, and Redundant or Discarded Features.

- **Correção de Provas por IA (AI Exam Correction):** *Score: 9.1/10 (High Spread: ±1.8)*. Recognized as the single most powerful driver of teacher buy-in. Teachers praised the ability to process large batches of student work against customizable rubrics. However, strict trust conditions apply: teachers insist on human oversight and question whether computer vision can handle messy student handwriting. Keep and prioritize; ensure human-in-the-loop review.
- **Perfis de Acessibilidade (Accessibility Profiles):** *Score: 8.9/10 (Moderate Spread: ±1.2)*. Deeply valued by specialized education (*AEE*) and general education teachers navigating mandated Individualized Educational Plans (*PEI*). Praise centered on structured differentiation for autism, ADHD, and dyslexia. Primary complaint stemmed from fear of AI hallucinations compromising pedagogical rigor. Keep and reinforce pedagogical guardrails.
- **Lições Mágicas (Magic Lessons):** *Score: 8.7/10 (Low Spread: ±0.9)*. The platform’s primary pedagogical copilot. Praised for generating full didactic sequences and formative descriptors. Widespread insistence that all sequences strictly align with the competencies of the National Common Curricular Base (*BNCC*). Keep as the central onboarding hook.
- **Assistente de IA do Professor (Teacher AI Assistant):** *Score: 8.1/10 (Moderate Spread: ±1.4)*. Highly valued for contextual pedagogical scaffolding, drafting conceptual analogies on the fly, and constructing formative rubrics. Teachers cautioned against generic outputs that mirror standard consumer chatbots. Keep and refine for educational specificity.
- **Geração de Avaliações (Assessment Generation):** *Score: 7.9/10 (Moderate Spread: ±1.3)*. Valued for diversifying formats (multiple choice, open-ended, and true/false items) tied to learning goals. Teachers voiced frustration when questions lacked regional context or required extensive post-editing. Keep with customizable item banks.
- **Digitalização de Manuscritos (Manuscript Digitization):** *Score: 6.8/10 (High Spread: ±2.4)*. Theoretically vital for schools where paper notebooks predominate, but heavily bottlenecked by uneven camera quality on low-end smartphones and erratic handwriting recognition. Keep only if offline edge-OCR improves; otherwise demote.
- **Geração de Slides (Slide Generation):** *Score: 6.4/10 (High Spread: ±2.1)*. Appreciated for rapid structuring, but teachers frequently noted that Canva Magic and Google Slides already serve this need well without added friction. Improve export versatility (native PPTX/PDF) or de-emphasize.
- **Sala de Aula (Virtual Classroom):** *Score: 5.2/10 (High Spread: ±2.6)*. Viewed by public school teachers as an unwelcome duplicate of Google Classroom or mandatory municipal digital diaries (*diários oficiais*). Discard standalone LMS ambitions; pivot to WhatsApp export and Google Classroom webhooks.
- **Aula ao Vivo (Live Class Mode):** *Score: 4.1/10 (Extreme Spread: ±3.1)*. The most rejected module in public school scenarios. Timed pacing and live AI assistance collapsed under spotty connectivity, absent digital projectors, and classrooms lacking individual student devices. Remove from core public-school workflows or relegate to an optional private-school utility.

**Core Value Anchors: The Uncompromising Drivers of Adoption**

The simulation demonstrates that teachers do not adopt EdTech for aspirational classroom modernization; they adopt it when it directly reclaims their personal well-being. Automated grading and accessibility structuring emerged as the platform's non-negotiable value anchors.

Educators routinely linked automated grading to the preservation of their personal lives, noting that handling crowded classrooms of 35 to 45 students across multiple school shifts leaves them functionally depleted:

> "The teacher is testing the AI exam grading and manuscript digitization of Magicboard and considers that the system could save weekends if it can read illegible handwriting and apply the rubric correctly for classes of 42 students."

However, teachers explicitly rejected fully autonomous grading, indicating that trust is granted only when the platform functions as an augmentative co-pilot rather than an autonomous judge:

> "The teacher believes that technology should automate administrative tasks such as slide assembly, note reading, and initial rubric application, freeing educators to focus on human teaching, mentoring, and mediation."

This sentiment was mirrored by pedagogical coordinators who emphasized that criteria-referenced rubrics must preserve the educator's final decision-making power:

> "The teacher considers that final teacher validation of the rubric is indispensable to accommodate creative and unexpected student answers."

Similarly, *Perfis de Acessibilidade* achieved near-unanimous acclaim among educators burdened by the complex paperwork of inclusive education (*LBI*). Rather than treating inclusion as an afterthought, teachers valued the platform’s capacity to translate Universal Design for Learning (*UDL*) into concrete adaptations:

> "The teacher in the resource room considers it encouraging to have a dedicated module for adapting pedagogical activities for autism, dyscalculia, and low vision."

Yet, this enthusiasm is tempered by an acute fear of algorithmic hallucination, particularly in official compliance documents:

> "The AEE teacher is concerned that AI hallucinations could compromise the rigor of individualized pedagogical adaptation in PEI documents."

For lesson planning (*Lições Mágicas*), the simulation establishes that adoption collapses if the tool produces generic global content. The platform achieved high marks specifically when its didactic sequencing remained anchored to national curriculum benchmarks:

> "A teacher wants to verify whether Magicboard content follows BNCC skills and works on mobile phones, given limited access to laptops and unstable school internet."

**Context-Dependent Utilities: Friction Under Material Constraints**

Modules categorized as context-dependent utilities revealed substantial operational variance based on school infrastructure and teacher digital literacy.

*Digitalização de Manuscritos* represents the most volatile utility. In state schools where notebooks remain the primary medium of instruction, teachers expressed strong desire for optical recognition:

> "The state-school teacher reports that Magicboard's digitization and personalized-rubric correction transformed the teacher's routine and freed weekends for direct student mentoring."

Yet in low-resource environments, attempts to scan dense or cursive handwriting on mid-range smartphones yielded high error rates, leading teachers to report that manually verifying OCR errors took more time than traditional grading. Furthermore, agents repeatedly highlighted strict compliance questions regarding student privacy and data sovereignty:

> "The teacher raised concerns about LGPD compliance and the cost of AI exam-correction tools for state-school teachers who cannot pay monthly fees from their own pockets."

Similarly, *Geração de Slides* and *Geração de Avaliações* occupied an ambiguous middle ground. While teachers appreciated automated formatting, many observed that these features duplicate existing workflows:

> "A teacher evaluates Magicboard based on BNCC compliance, direct operation on mobile phones, and the availability of laptops or stable internet in public schools."

When slides or quizzes could not be easily downloaded or shared directly via lightweight channels like WhatsApp, teachers defaulted back to free tools like Canva, ChatGPT, or pre-existing state repository materials.

**Discarded Utilities: The Collapse of Friction-Heavy Features**

The most decisive predictive signal from the simulation is the comprehensive rejection of *Aula ao Vivo* and *Sala de Aula* by public-network educators. 

*Aula ao Vivo* was engineered to provide real-time pacing, live AI assistance, and interactive student polling during the class period:

> "The live-class feature allows the teacher to follow the sequence of activities, check the planned time for each stage, and manage the rhythm of the session during class."

In actual public school simulation runs, this feature experienced severe failure rates. Teachers noted that maintaining an active, synchronized digital session in classrooms plagued by dropped Wi-Fi connections creates immediate classroom management crises. When the internet drops, the live stage freezes, paralyzing the lesson. Furthermore, teachers viewed timed instructional countdowns as pedagogically rigid, unable to accommodate the volatile behavioral and conceptual realities of crowded Brazilian classrooms. The feature proved viable only within well-funded private institutions with 1:1 student device infrastructure.

Similarly, *Sala de Aula* triggered intense resistance among educators who are already mandated by municipal and state education secretariats (*SEDUC/SME*) to log grades, attendance, and lesson logs in official bureaucratic systems:

> "The evaluation system and AI solutions should be contracted and integrated by school networks and sponsors, ensuring equitable access without individual financial burden on teachers."

Teachers refused to manage an isolated, third-party virtual classroom that would force them to engage in double data entry. They expressed zero willingness to maintain another closed portal, demanding instead that any student-facing output be exportable as lightweight PDF documents, printable evaluation sheets, or direct links sent to classroom WhatsApp groups. 

The simulated educator verdict is unequivocal: MagicBoard's viability in Brazil depends on stripping away synchronous, high-bandwidth utilities and doubling down on robust, mobile-optimized, asynchronous back-office relief.
